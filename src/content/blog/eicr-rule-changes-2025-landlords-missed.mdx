> **Quick answer:** On **1 November 2025** the electrical safety rules for landlords in England were amended by SI 2025/1043. The maximum civil penalty rose from **£30,000 to £40,000 per breach**, the next-inspection date on an EICR must be a **fixed calendar date**, and the "tenant refused access" defence now also covers refusals to people acting for you. Your existing valid EICR does not need replacing.

Most landlords learned the EICR rules in 2020 and 2021, when inspections first became mandatory for private lets in England. The rules have since been amended, and a lot of published guidance, including many letting agents' packs, still quotes the old figures.

## What changed on 1 November 2025

The amending regulations were primarily designed to bring social housing into the same electrical safety regime as the private rented sector. Along the way they made three changes that directly affect private landlords.

### 1. The penalty cap rose to £40,000

Regulation 10 of SI 2025/1043 substitutes £40,000 for £30,000 as the maximum financial penalty a council can impose. It is a per-breach figure. Failing to have an inspection carried out, failing to give the report to your tenant, and failing to complete remedial work within the deadline are separate duties, and each can attract its own penalty.

For breaches from **1 May 2026**, GOV.UK guidance directs councils to the civil penalty framework under the Renters' Rights Act 2025. Earlier breaches are dealt with under the Housing and Planning Act 2016 framework.

### 2. The next-inspection date must be a real date

An EICR must state when the next inspection is due. Some reports used to say things like "on change of occupancy". That no longer satisfies the rules: the next inspection has to be a fixed calendar date, and it cannot be more than five years after the inspection.

When your report arrives, check the date field. If it shows an event instead of a date, ask the electrician to reissue it.

### 3. The access defence is wider

Landlords have long had a defence where a tenant refuses access. The amendment extends that defence to refusals given to someone acting on the landlord's behalf. In practice, that is usually your electrician or managing agent.

The defence only works if you can prove you took all reasonable steps. Keep copies of every message, letter and missed appointment.

## What did not change

The core duties are the same as they were in 2020:

- Have the fixed electrical installation inspected and tested by a qualified person at least every **five years**, or sooner if the report says so.
- Give a copy of the report to existing tenants within **28 days** of the inspection, and to new tenants before they move in.
- Give a copy to the local council within **7 days** of a written request.
- Complete any remedial or further investigation work within **28 days**, or the shorter period the report specifies, and confirm completion in writing to the tenant and council within 28 days of the work.

A report is unsatisfactory if it contains any **C1**, **C2** or **FI** observation. C3 items are recommendations only.

## Why this matters more in London

A large share of London's rental stock is Victorian and Edwardian, often converted into flats with older wiring and, in some cases, more than one consumer unit. That combination raises the chance of C2 observations and of remedial deadlines being missed.

It also matters because of timing. The first compulsory inspections for existing tenancies were due by April 2021, so many of those five-year reports expire through 2026. A landlord who lets an EICR lapse this year faces a higher cap than in 2021.

## A practical checklist

1. Find your current EICR and note the next-inspection date.
2. If the date is within the next three months, book the renewal now.
3. Check the report states a calendar date, not an event.
4. Confirm you have evidence the tenant received a copy.
5. If there were C1, C2 or FI items, confirm the completion letter was sent.
