> **Quick answer** The same two-bed flat can be quoted £65 or £400 for an EICR, and both can be legitimate. The difference is almost never the qualification of the electrician. It's **how long they spend on site, what percentage of circuits they actually test, and how they make their money afterwards.** A thorough EICR on a small flat takes roughly 1.5 to 3 hours. Some firms run ten or more jobs a day. When the inspection fee barely covers the visit, the margin has to come from the remedial work. So the question worth asking isn't "why is this cheap". It's "what happens after the inspection".

You ring three electricians about the same flat in Hackney. One says £65. One says £150. One says he'd need to see it first but it'll probably be around £400.

Same flat. Same legal requirement. Same document at the end.

That's confusing, and it's the single most common thing landlords ask us about pricing. So here's the honest breakdown of what actually drives the number, written by a company that sits at the cheap end of that range and has no interest in pretending cheap is automatically bad.

## What actually changes an EICR price?

Six things. In rough order of how much they move the number.

| Factor | Effect on price | Why |
|--------|-----------------|-----|
| **Time on site** | Biggest single driver | A proper inspection of a one-bed takes 1.5 to 3 hours. Some quotes assume 45 minutes. |
| **Number of circuits** | £10 to £25 per circuit | Each circuit is tested individually. A studio might have 4. A five-bed house might have 14. |
| **Sampling percentage** | Large, and usually hidden | Testing 100% of accessories costs far more than testing 10%. Both are legal. The report has to declare which. |
| **Business model** | Large | A firm doing eight jobs a day in three boroughs has a completely different cost base from a sole trader crossing London for two. |
| **Location and access** | £0 to £30 | Congestion charge, ULEZ, parking. Some absorb it, some add it. |
| **Whether remedial work is the real product** | Can make the inspection almost free | A £65 inspection is a loss leader if a £1,500 remedial quote follows it. |

## The sampling percentage nobody tells you about

This is the part of an EICR most landlords never look at, and it explains more price variation than anything else.

Every EICR contains a limitations section. It declares what the inspector actually examined and what they didn't. An inspector might test 100% of circuits but only visually inspect 10% of the accessories (sockets, switches, light fittings). Another might do 25%. Both produce a valid report.

But they are not the same product, and they don't cost the same to produce.

So when you're comparing two quotes, the useful question is: **what percentage of accessories will be inspected, and will that be stated in the report?** An electrician who can't answer that quickly is telling you something.

> **Where to find it on your report** Look for a section titled **Extent and Limitations of Inspection and Testing**. It's usually on page 1 or 2, under the details of the installation. It should name the percentage sampled and any parts of the property that couldn't be accessed. If it's blank, or says "as agreed" with nothing agreed in writing, that's worth querying before you pay.

## Is a cheap EICR actually a problem?

Not inherently. And we'd say that even if we weren't at that end of the market, because the economics are genuinely different at volume.

Think about it from the electrician's side. A firm with eight jobs booked inside three boroughs has almost no travel cost per job, one admin process, and a predictable day. A sole trader driving from Croydon to Enfield for one inspection has to price in two hours of London traffic. Neither is cutting corners. They just have different overheads.

But there is a real risk with very low pricing, and it's worth naming.

Industry commentators have pointed out that when the inspection fee barely covers the visit, the incentive shifts to the remedial work. That's where the margin is. And remedial work is the part of the process where a landlord has the least ability to judge whether the recommendation is genuine.

So the price isn't the risk. The **remedial pipeline** is.

## How to protect yourself, whatever you pay

- **Ask whether you're obliged to use them for remedial work.** The right answer is no. If the answer is anything else, get a second quote before agreeing to anything.
- **Ask for the reason behind each C1 and C2, in writing.** "The wiring is old" is not a reason. Age alone doesn't make an installation unsafe, and it doesn't automatically justify a C2.
- **Ask how long the engineer will be on site** before they arrive. Then notice whether they were.
- **Read the limitations section** before you pay. That's where the sampling percentage lives.
- **Check the inspector's competent-person scheme number** on the NICEIC or NAPIT member finder. Note that scheme membership isn't legally mandatory for EICRs, but it's the easiest way to verify competence.
- **If a remedial quote feels heavy, get a second opinion on the report, not just the price.** A different electrician can tell you whether the codes themselves are justified.
