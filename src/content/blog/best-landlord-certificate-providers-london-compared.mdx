## The companies that won't tell you the price until you ring

Five of the nineteen London providers we checked publish no EICR, gas or EPC price anywhere on their site. Several more publish one or two and leave the rest blank, which is why four cells in the big table above read "not published".

That isn't automatically a red flag. Genuinely complex jobs (large HMOs, commercial units, listed buildings) do need a survey before anyone can quote honestly. But for a two-bed flat in Lewisham? There's no technical reason the number can't be on the page.

If you're calling a quote-only provider, ask these four things before you agree to anything:

- What's the total price for this exact property, including VAT?
- Is there a call-out fee, congestion charge, ULEZ charge or parking charge on top?
- Does the price change if the property is older, or has more circuits or appliances than expected?
- If the inspection fails, what do you charge for the remedial work, and am I obliged to use you for it?

That last one matters more than the first. A cheap inspection followed by an expensive, non-optional remedial quote is the most common way landlords end up paying far more than they expected.

## What about British Gas, HomeServe and the marketplaces?

You've got three other routes besides a London specialist. Each suits a different kind of landlord.

### Subscription cover (HomeServe, Hometree, British Gas)

HomeServe publishes £126 a year for a landlord gas plan that includes the annual inspection and CP12. Hometree includes the gas safety check and certificate in its core landlord cover plans, which start at £17.45 a month. It isn't free, it's bundled into a paid insurance policy. British Gas publishes no price at all and quotes by phone.

The maths is simple. A standalone CP12 at £49.99 costs you around £150 over three years. HomeServe's plan costs £378 over the same period. The £228 difference is buying you boiler breakdown cover, not a better certificate. Whether that's worth it depends entirely on the age of your boiler.

Worth knowing before you sign: HomeServe's landlord plan is Monday to Friday, 9 to 5 appointments only, and covers a maximum of two boilers.

### Marketplaces (Checkatrade, MyBuilder, Bark, MyConstructor)

These aren't providers. They're introducers. You post the job, trades bid, you pick one.

MyConstructor is the most polished of them for certificates, with instant Greater London pricing (EICR £69 studio, EPC £34 studio), online booking in under a minute and a very large public review base. On raw price for a single certificate it's hard to beat.

Where marketplaces get awkward is multi-certificate jobs and what happens after a fail. You'll typically need separate trades for gas, electrical and EPC, which means three appointments and three access arrangements. And if a job goes wrong, you're chasing an individual trade rather than a company with an operations team.

## Is a £65 EICR safe to use, or are you buying a problem?

Fair question. And the honest answer has two halves.

Half one: yes, a [properly conducted EICR](/blog/what-is-an-eicr) at £65 to £70 is entirely legitimate in London. The reason isn't corner-cutting, it's route density. A company doing eight jobs a day inside the same three boroughs has a completely different cost base from a one-van electrician driving across the city for two.

Half two: a low inspection fee does create an incentive problem, and you should know about it. Industry commentators have pointed out that a thorough inspection of a flat takes around three hours, while some firms are doing ten or more jobs a day. If the inspection itself barely breaks even, the money has to come from somewhere. Usually that's the remedial work.

So the question isn't "is £65 too cheap". It's "what happens after the inspection".

> **Five questions that tell you more than the price does**
>
> **1. How long will the engineer be on site?** A one-bed flat should take 1.5 to 3 hours. Twenty minutes is not an EICR.
>
> **2. What percentage of circuits will be tested, and where is that declared?** Every EICR has a limitations section. Read it.
>
> **3. Am I obliged to use you for any remedial work?** The right answer is no.
>
> **4. Will you give me a written breakdown of any C1 and C2 items with the reason for each code?** "Old wiring" is not a reason.
>
> **5. Can I have the inspector's competent-person scheme number?** You can then check it yourself on the NICEIC or NAPIT member finder.

## How to check any London provider before you book

Five minutes of checking beats a week of arguing later. Here's the sequence.

- **Check the engineer, not just the company.** For gas, search the engineer's name or licence number on the Gas Safe Register. For electrical work, use the NICEIC or NAPIT member finder. A company saying it's "NICEIC approved" isn't the same as the individual attending being registered.
- **Ask for a registration number, not a logo.** Any firm can put a badge on a website. Registration numbers are checkable.
- **Check whether the price is the price.** Ask specifically about call-out fees, parking, congestion charge, ULEZ and older-property surcharges. These are where a fixed price stops being fixed.
- **Ask when you get the certificate.** "Within 24 hours of inspection" and "within 24 hours of payment" are not the same promise.
- **Look for a real address and a company number.** Several London certificate sites publish neither. That doesn't make them dishonest, but it does make them hard to chase.

### The legionella point that matters more than the price

The Health and Safety Executive is unambiguous about this. Its guidance for landlords states that health and safety law does not require landlords to obtain or produce a legionella test certificate. The legal duty is to assess and control the risk, and HSE says most landlords can carry out that assessment themselves without being trained or accredited.

So if a managing agent or contractor has sent you a letter demanding an annual "legionella certificate" and quoting a fine, check it against the HSE's own page before you pay anyone, including us.

Where a paid assessment genuinely earns its money is stored water systems, tanks, larger HMOs, commercial premises and properties with vulnerable occupants. For a standard two-bed flat with a combi boiler, it usually doesn't.
